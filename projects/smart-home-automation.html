Smart Home Automation

Control nine devices by voice or dashboard, schedule them in plain English, and watch energy draw live – built as three independent processes talking over a message broker.

Architecture

0_mqtt_simulator.py

Stands in for hardware. Holds the nine devices with their real wattages and publishes energy telemetry on a loop.

1_voice_automation.py

Listens, transcribes, and turns free-form English into device commands and schedules.

2_web_bridge.py

Runs an HTTP server and an MQTT client side by side, exposing the system to a browser dashboard.

The three know almost nothing about each other – they communicate only by publishing and subscribing to topics, so any one can be restarted, replaced or moved to another machine without the others noticing.

The Problem

Smart-home devices are fragmented. Every brand ships its own app and cloud account, so a house with lights from one vendor and a thermostat from another has no single place to control anything – and no view of energy use at all.

So the design question was never "how do I switch a light on". It was: what messaging architecture lets devices of any type join one system without rewriting the controller each time?

Approach

STEP 01

Publish/subscribe over direct calls

MQTT was chosen precisely so components would not hold references to each other. Wildcard topic subscriptions mean a new device publishing on the agreed pattern is picked up automatically – the bridge needs no change to support it. That is the property that answers the fragmentation problem.

STEP 02

Intent parsing as a dispatch table

Rather than a chain of string comparisons, speech is matched against an ordered table of 14 regular-expression patterns, each bound to a handler. Adding a capability means adding one row. The table covers on/off control, temperature, status queries, energy reporting, four phrasings of scheduling, three of cancellation, and help.

STEP 03

Compound and time-aware commands

A command containing and is split and each half recursively re-parsed, so "turn off the kitchen light and turn on the fan" executes as two actions. Scheduling accepts relative time ("in 30 minutes") and absolute time ("at 7 pm"), and tasks can be listed and cancelled by index or by "the last one".

STEP 04

Concurrency in the bridge

The bridge serves HTTP and consumes MQTT at the same time. The HTTP server runs on a daemon thread alongside the MQTT event loop, with CORS pre-flight handled explicitly so the browser dashboard can call it. Endpoints cover /api/voice-command, /api/device/toggle and /api/device/add.

STEP 05

Bounded energy history

Every telemetry message appends to an in-memory history capped at 2,880 samples – a full day at 30-second resolution – with the oldest dropped on overflow. A long-running process cannot grow without bound, which matters more on a Raspberry Pi than on a laptop.

Challenges

01

Security gaps in my own design

Once it worked, I reviewed it as an attacker rather than as its author. The dashboard has a login screen, but the REST endpoints behind it never check who is calling – anyone who can reach the port controls every device, so the authentication was decoration. MQTT traffic is also unencrypted, so anyone on the network can read telemetry or publish forged commands.

Both are documented with a prioritised fix plan rather than quietly left out. Finding them was the most useful part of the project.

02

Speech recognition is unreliable by nature

Transcription fails constantly – ambient noise, timeouts, network errors, audio that is simply unintelligible. Each failure mode is caught separately and returns a distinct message rather than a stack trace, and the recogniser calibrates for ambient noise before each listen. A text command path runs alongside the voice path so the system stays testable without a microphone.

03

Ambiguous phrasing

People do not speak in one fixed grammar. "Schedule the fan to turn off at 9", "set the fan to turn off at 9" and "turn off the fan at 9" mean the same thing and match different expressions. Pattern order matters, because a broad pattern placed too early swallows commands meant for a narrower one. When nothing matches, a keyword-and-device-name fallback tries to recover intent instead of giving up.

Result

What works

  • Voice and dashboard control of 9 devices
  • Compound commands in one utterance
  • Relative and absolute scheduling
  • Live energy aggregation
  • New devices join without controller changes

Known limitations

  • Devices are simulated, not physical
  • REST endpoints are unauthenticated
  • MQTT transport is unencrypted
  • History is in-memory; a restart clears it
